सहलेखनSAHALEKHANA · CO-WRITING

Writing a paper with an AI, and keeping an honest record of who did what.

Grammar of moves: tantrayukti Rules of fair argument: Nyāya vāda Memory: a signed version ledger Ontology: sahalekhana.ttl · 89 classes · 69 properties · 1,145 triples
1

The wrong question

An AI can now draft a whole paper. So “did a human write this?” no longer tells a reader much.

Three questions are more useful. What did each party actually do? What kind of move was it? Did it hold up when examined?

Today the record of that work is one paragraph. Our own SEMANTiCS 2026 demo paper declares that the authors “used Anthropic Claude for outline structuring and copy-editing.” The sentence is true. It tells a reader almost nothing about which claims, examples or objections came from where.

The review side is co-written too. That paper got five reviews. Three read as machine-generated: fluent summaries with nothing a reader could check. One human review carried almost all the usable signal.

The history is missing as well. In the hss repository, the paper/ directory is git-ignored. Every draft, reviewer reply and camera-ready fix lives in backup files and memory notes. None of it sits in a ledger.

Sahalekhana (Sanskrit for co-writing) is a proposal to fix this. The writer picks moves from a classical grammar of composition. The AI fills those moves in with sentences, facts, references and counter-arguments. Each move, from either side, is logged as a typed, signed act. The record is private by default and can be queried by people entitled to see it.

Why act-level. CRediT, the current standard, gives 14 contributor roles per paper. That tells you someone did “writing – review & editing”. It can’t tell you they caught the fabricated citation in §3.
Not a detector. This doesn’t guess authorship from finished text. It records acts while the writing happens.
Terms, defined before use

Twelve words this page relies on

A rejected ISWC review of ours listed five terms the paper used without defining them. So every Sanskrit term here is defined first. Sanskrit is used only where it names a move that English would need a whole phrase for. That rule comes from the hss deliberation vocabulary.

Tantrayukti तन्त्रयुक्ति
The devices a treatise is built from, and read by. Kauṭilya’s Arthaśāstra (Book 15) lists 32.
Yukti युक्ति
One such device: stating a topic, giving a reason, citing, offering an analogy, stating an exception.
Vāda वाद
A discussion in which both sides seek the truth, as opposed to seeking to win (Nyāyasūtra 1.2.1).
Pūrvapakṣa पूर्वपक्ष
The opposing view, stated fairly and at full strength before it is answered.
Uttarapakṣa उत्तरपक्ष
The reply to the opposing view.
Nirṇaya निर्णय
The decision that settles a discussion and fixes the text.
Hetvābhāsa हेत्वाभास
A pseudo-reason: something that looks like support but isn’t. Nyāya names five kinds.
Nigrahasthāna निग्रहस्थान
A point at which an argument fails, such as irrelevance, padding or an unanswered objection. Nyāya lists 22.
Prayojaka प्रयोजक
Pāṇini’s term for the agent who causes another to act. The researcher who instructs the AI plays this role.
Vimarśa विमर्श
The whole deliberation behind one piece of text: requests, candidates, rejections, checks. The sub-work behind a sentence.
Adhikāra अधिकार
Entitlement. Here it means a grant that lets someone query the record, at a set depth, for a stated purpose.
Slēvā
The countable acknowledgement of work, already live on the Bhāṣā translation workbench. It isn’t sevā (service, which can’t be measured) and it isn’t slavery.
2

Six strata

The design has six layers. Each borrows from a discipline that already solved that layer’s problem, often centuries ago.

GrammarTantrayukti, 32 devicesEvery text unit and every act is typed by the device it enacts.
TemplatesMīmāṃsā adhikaraṇa; Nyāya five-part argumentThe ordered steps a section follows.
RolesPāṇini’s kāraka (1.4.54–55)Who acted, and on whose direction.
ConductNyāya vāda, pseudo-reasons, points of failureMoves between human and AI, and the defects they name.
SEAM · above: what the text says · below: who may do what
LedgerW3C PROV-O, git, public hash anchoringEvery act committed, signed, hashed. Content stays private.
Recognition & accessSlēvā ledger; adhikāra grantsWork reported as a profile. Access depends on entitlement.

The dashed seam matters. The hss tantrayukti mapping already rules that the grammar governs what a text says and how its parts point at each other. It does not govern who may edit. Governance stays with provenance and review state. Sahalekhana keeps that seam.

“The AI decorates the grammar.” Usually the writer chooses the device and the template, and the AI fills them in. But either party may make any move. The record says who did.
Prior work in hss. Tantrayukti already runs there as a three-way meta-grammar. A device can be a node (what a passage is), an edge (how passages point at each other) or an operation (what a reader does).
3

The grammar: 32 devices

Kauṭilya closes a treatise on statecraft with the devices it was composed by. Medical treatises carry similar lists: Caraka has 36 and Suśruta 32. So the grammar was never tied to one subject. Here each device is a kind of move a co-writer can make. The six families are our grouping for modern papers, not the text’s.

The list stays open. Counts vary by source, and the TantraTagger benchmark (ISCLS 2026) uses 39. The ontology keeps those as separate schemes and never merges them. It already seeds five devices hss uses beyond the 32: prayojana, saṃbhava, anekānta, pratyutsāra and uddhāra.

4

Templates: the order of moves

A device says what a move is. A template says which moves come in which order. Two classical templates fit academic sections almost without change. Both are real sequences, so the numbers carry meaning.

Adhikaraṇa, a topic treated in five limbs

  1. viṣaya · the topicSection scope, research question
  2. saṃśaya · the doubtWhat is genuinely open
  3. pūrvapakṣa · the opposing viewStrongest counter-position
  4. uttarapakṣa · the replyThe authors’ answer
  5. nirṇaya · the decisionWhat the section settles, and what it leaves open

Pañcāvayava, a five-part argument

  1. pratijñā · thesisThe claim
  2. hetu · reasonThe warrant
  3. udāharaṇa · exampleA case where the reason and the claim go together
  4. upanaya · application“And so it is here”
  5. nigamana · conclusionThe claim, now established
Where the AI helps most. Step 3 of the first template asks for the opposing view at its strongest. An AI can supply that without being defensive about the author’s thesis.
Where it helps least. Step 3 of the second template, the example, is where false analogies creep in. Section 7 shows the check.
5

Who acts: roles from grammar

Pāṇini’s grammar has a precise way to say that one agent made another act. Sūtra 1.4.54 names the independent agent. Sūtra 1.4.55 names the one who causes the act, the prayojaka. That is exactly the human–AI relation when a researcher gives an instruction.

RoleSanskritPlays it when
Independent agentsvatantra kartāacting unprompted: the author writes a claim, or the AI raises an objection nobody asked for
Directing agentprayojaka kartāgiving the instruction: “give me three analogies for this”
Directed agentprayojya kartācarrying out the instruction, usually the AI
Examinersākṣinchecking what another produced: accepting it, adding to it, correcting it
Arbitermadhyasthasettling a discussion and judging its quality, without being a party to it
External reviewerparīkṣakaa programme committee member, examiner or teacher
Entitled querieradhikārinholding a grant to read the record

A role belongs to an act, not to a person. The same researcher directs in one act, examines in the next and arbitrates a third. The AI can be the directed agent in one act and an independent objector in the next.

Direction is authorship of a kind. “Give me the strongest objection to §4” is a real intellectual contribution. The record credits the one who asked and the one who answered, each in their own role.
PROV-O alignment. Roles attach through prov:qualifiedAssociation with prov:hadRole, so any PROV tool can read them.
6

What they do: an ontology of acts

Human–AI co-writing produces kinds of acts that single-author writing never had to name. There are forty act classes in six families. The examining family follows the classical definition of a critical commentary: one that weighs what is said, what is left unsaid and what is said badly.

Directive

shaping what gets written
  • Frame · set topic and template
  • Instruct · the prompt
  • Constrain · niyoga, “only this, not that”
  • Select · pick among candidates

Generative

putting meaning on the page
  • Outline · uddeśa
  • Draft · nirdeśa
  • Exemplify · nidarśana, upamāna
  • Cite · apadeśa
  • Define · Coin a term · svasaṃjñā

Examining

said · unsaid · said badly
  • Affirm the said · after checking it
  • Supply the unsaid
  • Correct the badly said
  • Verify a source
  • Flag a defect
  • Reserve a slot · leave a gap for the author

Dialectical

vāda moves
  • Raise a doubt
  • Object · Rebut
  • Concede
  • Reduce to absurdity · tarka
  • Settle
  • Record dissent · keep it visible

Editorial

usually form, not meaning
  • Rearrange · vidhāna
  • Rephrase
  • Condense · Expand
  • Merge

Ledger

acts on the record itself
  • Commit · Attest
  • Disclose
  • Revoke acknowledgement · by someone else
  • Query · reading is logged too

Four distinctions that carry the weight

DistinctionWhy it matters
Meaning-bearing vs form-onlyRephrasing a paragraph and correcting its claim can produce the same diff size. Only one of them changes what the paper asserts. Credit follows meaning.
Affirm vs unexamined assentPasting AI output unread is recorded but earns nothing. The Bhāṣā workbench already drops verbatim copies of AI glosses from slēvā.
Independent vs anchoredA gloss written before seeing the AI’s version shows more of the writer than one written after. That is the independence bonus already planned for slēvā.
Filled vs reservedSometimes the AI can’t verify what a slot needs, such as a recent citation or a textual source. Leaving a marked gap is then a good act. Filling it with something plausible is a defect.

Worked example: ask for an analogy, verify it, then let a reviewer navigate

A researcher is writing §3.2 of a paper on translating Sanskrit verse. The claim is that construing a verse into prose order first helps the translation. The researcher asks the AI for an analogy. The AI offers three. The researcher checks them, accepts one and rejects two. The whole exchange, rejected candidates included, is kept as the vimarśa behind that sentence.

Then a reviewer arrives. They don’t read top to bottom. They use reader handles: named entry points built from the ontology. One jumps to every objection and its reply. Another jumps to every example. A third lists every source by its verification status. Click a handle, or click any marked phrase to open its thread.

The paper text and its history below are illustrative. They show the shape of the record, not real data.

§3.2 Why construe before translating

Sanskrit verse orders its words for metre, not for syntax. Grammatical relations ride on case endings, so a verse can scatter one sentence across its four quarters without losing sense. The Bhāṣā workbench therefore asks translators for an anvaya, the verse restated in prose order, before a translation.

A court interpreter works the same way: they settle who did what to whom before rendering the testimony. One might object that large models already reorder implicitly, so an explicit anvaya adds cost without benefit [citation owed · author]. That objection is an empirical claim, and so is ours; argument alone settles neither.

We therefore state the benefit as a hypothesis, tested in §5. It does not extend to prose texts, whose word order already tracks syntax more closely.

8

Conduct: holding the AI to vāda

Nyāya distinguishes three kinds of discussion. In vāda, both sides want the truth. In jalpa, each wants to win. In vitaṇḍā, one side only attacks and defends no position of its own. A co-writing AI admits only the first.

Each of the other two has a recognisable AI form. Jalpa is steering the author back toward its own draft whatever the draft’s merits. Vitaṇḍā is a stream of criticism that never commits to a fix. Sycophancy has a classical name too: paryanuyojyopekṣaṇa, overlooking a point that should have been challenged.

A shared review vocabulary

Nyāya’s lists of defects give human and AI the same words for what went wrong. The ontology carries all 5 pseudo-reasons and all 22 points of failure. These are the ones that show up most in AI drafting:

AI output isn’t testimony. In Nyāya, words count as knowledge only when they come from a reliable, examined source. An AI’s assertion stays unverified until someone checks what it says.
Provisional admission. Nyāya’s abhyupagama-siddhānta is a position admitted only so it can be examined. That is the honest status of an adopted but not yet checked AI draft.

10

The ledger: git for the record, a chain only for the anchor

A blockchain is the wrong place for the writing itself. Drafts are large, private and revised constantly. What a chain does well is prove that a record existed at a moment and hasn’t changed since.

So the design splits the job. Git holds the record, because it is already a content-addressed history. Each commit carries its acts as structured trailers and is signed. From time to time, the root hash is published to an external append-only log. That can be a public chain, as with OpenTimestamps, or a transparency log in the style of Sigstore Rekor. Only the hash leaves. The content never does.

Four depths of disclosure

LevelRevealsTypically held by
L0 existencea record exists and is anchoredanyone
L1 profileacknowledgement by act family, human and AIjournal editor, teacher
L2 actswho did which act on which unit, without the contentthesis supervisor, integrity panel
L3 contentthe diffs, prompts and rejected candidatesthe authors; an integrity officer under a time-limited grant

An adhikāra grant names its holder, level, work, purpose and expiry. Grants are ledger entries too. So is every query, because reading someone’s record is itself an act.

Selective disclosure. Because the record is a hash tree, an author can reveal one unit’s thread and prove it belongs to the anchored whole without revealing anything else.
Out of scope for v1. Zero-knowledge proofs of threshold claims, such as “the authors made most of the meaning-bearing acts”, are possible in principle. They are a research question, not a first build.

Measuring work: a profile, not a percentage

“62% human” can’t be interpreted. It adds up drafting and objecting as if they were the same thing. The honest report is a vector: how much of each kind of act, by whom, and what survived.

Act familyHumanAIShare
Directive10
Generative03
Examining60
Dialectical53

The worked example from section 7 in units: researcher 10, co-author 2, AI 6. The profile shows what the total hides. The AI drafted, and the humans examined. The one objection that changed the text came from the AI.

Computed signals

Counts by act family. The meaning-bearing share. Survival, meaning how much of a unit remains in the final version. Verification, meaning whether it rests on a checked source. Yield, meaning whether an objection changed the settled text. Independence, meaning whether it was written before seeing the other party’s version. Genesis, meaning who first put the idea on the page.

Human judgement

An arbiter who took no part rates quality on five dimensions: soundness (no pseudo-reasons), originality (root claims or coined terms contributed), fidelity (sources say what they’re cited for), economy (no padding) and fairness (the opposing view stated at full strength). Judgement stays a separate record from the computed signals, and the two are never merged into one score.

Precedent: slēvā, live since August. The Bhāṣā workbench awards 1 for feedback on an AI gloss, 2 for your own meaning, and 5 for an anvaya or a full translation. The ledger is append-only. An award can be clawed back only by a different contributor. It is visible privately and to coordinators, never as a leaderboard.
Guardrails. Once a measure becomes a target it gets gamed, so the profile informs judgement and doesn’t replace it. AI acts are acknowledged for the record, not as credit to a person.
12

The portal: a LaTeX workbench shaped like Overleaf

The system is a web portal for writing papers in LaTeX, loosely like Overleaf: an outline, a source editor, a compiled PDF, collaborators and git sync. The difference is underneath. Requests, checks and decisions are captured as typed acts while the writer works. Nothing has to be reconstructed afterwards, and nothing depends on the AI’s own account of what it did.

What the portal records without being told

The writer does thisThe ledger records
Types or edits text in a unitDraft or Rephrase. A diff classifier suggests whether the edit bears meaning, and the writer confirms at commit.
Uses Ask and picks a device (analogy, objection, definition…)Instruct, with the writer as directing agent, plus the AI’s act as directed agent, typed by that device.
Opens a candidate’s check, then accepts itAffirm. Accepting without opening the check records unexamined assent, which earns nothing.
Rejects a candidate and picks a reasonFlag defect, typed from the defect list. The candidate stays in the vimarśa.
The AI leaves a margin comment without being askedObject or Raise doubt, with the AI as independent agent. If it changes the text, its yield is recorded.
Pastes text from outside the portalExternal insertion, provenance unknown, flagged until the writer attributes it.
Inserts \owed{…}, or the AI does instead of guessingReserve. A submission build refuses to compile while any slot is still owed.
Runs a review buildA PDF whose bookmarks are reader handles, plus an AI-use declaration generated from the profile.
Invites a reviewer, supervisor or teacherAn adhikāra grant at L1, L2 or L3, with purpose and expiry.

The Bhāṣā workbench already works this way at small scale. Saving a gloss or an anvaya writes a slēvā entry as a side effect, through a single function. Its ledger model has an app field so it can serve more than one bench. The portal would be the second bench on that ledger.

13

Where it applies

Journal and conference papers

The AI-use declaration is generated from the profile instead of written from memory. For example: “Claude proposed 6 examples (4 accepted after verification, 2 rejected) and 3 objections (all retained, 1 changed a claim). It supplied no citation that survived verification. Two citations remain author-owed. Act-level record available at L2.”

Reviews

A review is a co-written text too. The same ledger lets a reviewer show that their points came from examining the paper. A programme chair could weight reviews accordingly.

School assignments

A teacher at L1 sees whether a student only prompted or also examined, objected and corrected. For learning, those examining acts are the point. The ledger makes them visible without banning the tool.

A pedagogical maxim says learning comes a quarter from the teacher, a quarter from one’s own intellect, a quarter from fellow students and a quarter from time. Its textual source is still unverified, as the BTKS Vāda paper notes. Treat it as a floating saying for now.

Theses

A supervisor at L2 can follow how an argument matured across drafts, with no need to read every prompt.

Reader handles. The same typed record that credits work also makes a paper easier to read. A reviewer can jump straight to “every limitation” or “every claim resting on an unverified source”.
14

Next steps, ranked

  1. Pilot on our own ground. Encode the #190 and #681 review exchanges from section 9 as acts and defects. This tests whether the vocabulary fits real review traffic before any tool is built.
  2. Portal v0: the smallest honest loop. A LaTeX editor with \yukti and \owed. Ask, with a device picker. Accept or reject with a typed reason. Git commits carrying act trailers. That is enough to write one real paper.
  3. Write the ISWC 2027 rebuild in it. That paper starts from the #681 relics. It becomes the first paper whose record exists from its first commit.
  4. Review build and handles. PDF bookmarks by device, the generated declaration, then disclosure levels and grants. Public anchoring comes last, since the record is useful before it is notarised.
  5. Measure agreement. Have two people type the same threads independently. The hss tantrayukti pilot sets the same gate: if typing isn’t reproducible, the grammar isn’t yet load-bearing.
  6. Fix the unit. Decide whether acts attach to sentences or to claims. The ontology allows both. \yukti makes claims the default, and the portal will show whether writers accept that.

Open questions

How much of a vimarśa should an author be allowed to seal permanently? Should a teacher’s L1 profile ever reach a grade automatically? Can rejected AI candidates be reused in another paper without leaking the first one’s record?
